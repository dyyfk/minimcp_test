\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Spoken interaction leaves little time to choose a response
\citep{stivers2009universals}. One practical design pairs a local
speech model that keeps the conversation going with a stronger expert
for hard questions. Calling the expert on every turn adds cost and
waiting time. Selective escalation requires an estimate of local-answer
failure risk that is available when the model begins to respond.

Prior work gives two starting points. For text models, hidden-state
probes and verbal self-evaluation can predict whether an answer is
correct \citep{kadavath2022language,azaria2023internal,kossen2024semantic},
and intermediate layers can carry information that the output does
not show \citep{orgad2024llms}. Text routers use such signals to
divide queries between models of different cost
\citep{chen2023frugalgpt,ong2024routellm,varshney2026llmrouter}.
For speech, native duplex models decide for themselves when to listen
and when to speak \citep{defossez2024moshi,openbmb2026minicpmo}.

Three things remain unknown. First, it is not known whether a failure
probe still works when the query pool changes; a high in-distribution
AUC can come from pool identity rather than from competence. Second,
it is not known whether a probe fit on text input still reads
speech-input states after speech and duplex fine-tuning. Third, it is
not known where in a streaming protocol the hidden state is actually
available for a routing decision. A duplex model already decides when
to speak, but this floor-management decision need not reveal whether
it knows the answer. Although the pretrained backbone can carry
competence information, its accessibility across layers, read positions,
and input modalities remains unclear in native streaming speech
after duplex fine-tuning.

This gap matters in practice. A gate placed at the wrong layer can
look good on its calibration pool and then invert on a new one, so
the expert is called on the wrong turns. A probe that fails on audio
input cannot be used in a speech system at all. And a read that only
becomes available after the answer is generated removes the early
decision that motivated escalation in the first place.

We study these questions on frozen MiniCPM-o~4.5
\citep{openbmb2026minicpmo}, with raw-backbone and other speech-model
controls. We make three contributions. First, a layer and position
audit shows that the accessibility of failure information depends on
the input and calibration regime, motivating our L22 read
(Figure~\ref{fig:layersweep}). A matched native ablation separates
the contribution of feature aggregation from layer choice
(Figure~\ref{fig:native-ablation}). Second, text-trained probes
transfer to speech-input states, reaching 0.760 AUC on query-disjoint
human recordings (Figure~\ref{fig:xmodal-transfer}). Cross-model
controls test the breadth of this transfer, which remains distinct
from the calibration needed for native serving. Third, a separately
fitted answer-onset gate raises internal answer-content accuracy by
22.1 percentage points and exceeds the reported random-mixture
reference by 6.8 points at the aggressive tier. An independent
real-time replay measures server waiting time after input end to
first answer audio. These unjudged timing sessions do not establish
joint accuracy--latency gains or client-audible delay.
