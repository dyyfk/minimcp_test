\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
As a spoken question arrives, a conversational agent must decide both
when to respond and whether it can answer reliably. A local speech
model can manage the exchange, while a stronger expert can help with
questions beyond its competence. Routine expert calls also make
locally answerable questions incur the cost and wait of a handoff.
The useful intervention is selective: retain local
answers when they are likely to succeed and request help when they
are likely to fail. This requires a signal available at the moment
the speech model begins to respond.

Native duplex models already decide when to listen and when to speak
\citep{defossez2024moshi,openbmb2026minicpmo}. That ability creates a
natural place to intervene, but the decision to take the floor need
not reveal whether the model knows the answer. A model can be ready
to speak while lacking the knowledge or reasoning needed for a correct
response. Waiting until it has finished an answer would allow a later
check, but would also postpone expert help.

Text models offer a starting point. Hidden-state probes and verbal
self-evaluation can predict answer correctness
\citep{kadavath2022language,azaria2023internal,kossen2024semantic}, and
intermediate layers can expose information absent from the output
\citep{orgad2025llms}. Text routers allocate queries across models of
different cost \citep{chen2023frugalgpt,ong2024routellm,varshney2026llmrouter}.
In native speech, the model receives an evolving audio context, and
the state used for routing must be produced by its
listen/speak protocol. A useful text-input signal may change after
speech and duplex fine-tuning, or become inaccessible at the point
where a serving system needs it.

We ask whether failure information remains readable across these
changes and can support an actionable handoff. Generalization matters:
a probe may distinguish familiar query pools without recognizing
failure on a new kind of question. Modality matters because a probe
fit on text must be tested on speech-input states, including unseen
human recordings. Readout design matters because the layer, position,
and aggregation determine what information the gate receives. These
questions connect representation analysis to deployment: a predictive
score is useful only if it remains informative in the intended input
regime and arrives in time to guide the response.

We study frozen MiniCPM-o \citep{openbmb2026minicpmo}, with
raw-backbone and other speech-model controls. Our layer and position
audit traces how failure readout depends on the input and calibration
regime (Figure~\ref{fig:layersweep}); a matched native ablation
separates aggregation from layer choice
(Figure~\ref{fig:native-ablation}). Text-trained probes also transfer
to speech-input states on unseen human recordings
(Figure~\ref{fig:xmodal-transfer}). To turn this evidence into a
serving decision, we separately fit an aggregated answer-onset gate
on native audio features. It reuses the local model's hidden states
without updating the model or waiting for a completed answer. Lower-risk
turns continue locally; higher-risk turns request an expert answer
that the local talker then voices.

The resulting handoff improves answer-content accuracy and, at the
aggressive tier, exceeds the matched random-mixture reference. This
connects readable failure information to selective expert use, while
leaving an essential conversational question open: whether the improved
answer is worth the interruption and wait. We report the judged
answer-content benchmark alongside the full real-time timing evaluation
to compare accuracy and server waiting time across routing policies.
User-perceived delay and interruption quality remain to be evaluated.
